Module 2 — 8 to 20 Minutes

How AI & LLMs Actually Work
— No Jargon

Before writing a single prompt, students need a mental model of what AI is "thinking." This module builds that model using everyday analogies.

The Simple Truth About AI

One-Line Explanation

AI learns patterns from millions of examples and uses those patterns to make predictions.

That's it. Everything else is details.

🍕 Analogy 1: The Pizza Chef

Imagine a chef who has eaten 10 million pizzas from around the world. Now when you describe a pizza ("thin crust, spicy, vegetarian"), they instantly know what ingredients to use — not because they memorized a recipe, but because they've absorbed patterns from millions of experiences.

AI works the same way. It "ate" (trained on) billions of words, images, or data points. Now when you give it input, it pattern-matches to generate the best response.

What Is a Large Language Model (LLM)?

What LLMs Are Good At
  • Writing and summarizing text
  • Answering questions
  • Writing and explaining code
  • Translating languages
  • Brainstorming ideas
  • Analyzing documents
What LLMs Struggle With
  • Real-time information (they have a cutoff date)
  • Precise math calculations
  • 100% factual accuracy always
  • Knowing private/internal data
  • Physical world interaction

How Training Works — The "Autocomplete Monster" Analogy

📱 Analogy 2: Phone Autocomplete × 1000

You know how your phone suggests the next word when you're typing? It learned from your texts. Now imagine that same idea but:

  • Instead of your texts → it read all of Wikipedia, every book, most of the internet
  • Instead of 3 word suggestions → it can write entire essays, code, and stories
  • Instead of a phone chip → it uses thousands of specialized processors (GPUs) for months

The result: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini — these are ultra-powerful autocomplete systems that understand context, instructions, and nuance.

The Training Process (Simplified)

  1. Collect data: Gather billions of sentences, articles, books from the internet
  2. Show patterns: "The cat sat on the ___" → model predicts "mat" (or "floor", "chair")
  3. Measure error: Was the prediction good? By how much was it wrong?
  4. Adjust weights: Tiny internal adjustments to improve next prediction (happens billions of times)
  5. Repeat millions of times: The model slowly gets better and better at understanding language
  6. Human feedback: Real humans rate responses. Model learns to be helpful, not just "correct"

Critical Concept: AI Is Not "Thinking"

AI doesn't "understand" like humans do. It's an extremely sophisticated pattern matcher. It generates the most statistically likely response based on its training. This is why you should always verify important AI outputs — it can be confidently wrong.

What Is a Prompt?

Definition

A prompt is the instruction or question you give to an AI. Prompt Engineering is the skill of crafting prompts that get you exactly what you want — reliably and efficiently.

Think of it as: Learning how to "talk" to the most powerful assistant in history.

The 5 Golden Rules of Prompting

Rule 1 — Be Specific & Detailed
❌ Weak Prompt
Write about climate change.
Problem: Too vague. What format? What audience? What angle? AI will guess and likely miss.
✅ Strong Prompt
Write a 200-word summary of climate change causes for a 10th-grade student. Use simple language, 3 bullet points, and end with one thing they can do to help.
Why: Specific length, audience, format, and action item. AI knows exactly what you want.
Rule 2 — Assign a Role / Persona

Tell the AI WHO to be. This dramatically improves quality because it sets context for the entire response.

❌ Without Role
Explain machine learning.
Generic textbook answer. Could be for anyone.
✅ With Role
You are a friendly data science teacher. Explain machine learning to a complete beginner using only food analogies. No technical jargon.
Specific persona + audience + constraint = targeted, useful output.
Rule 3 — Provide Context & Examples

The more context you give, the better the output. If you want a specific style, show an example.

✅ With Context + Example
I'm applying for a Data Analyst job at a bank. I have 3 years of Excel experience and just completed a Python course. Here is my current summary: "I am good with data." Rewrite this summary in a professional, confident tone. Here's an example of the style I want: "Results-driven analyst with 5 years transforming complex datasets into business insights..."
Rule 4 — Specify the Output Format

AI can output in many formats. Tell it exactly what you want.

Format RequestExample Use Case
"Give me a numbered list"Steps to follow
"Format as a table with columns: X, Y, Z"Comparison data
"Write as bullet points under 3 headings"Report sections
"Output only the Python code, no explanation"Code generation
"Give me exactly 5 options, one per line"Brainstorming
"Respond in JSON format"For developers/apps
Rule 5 — Iterate! Don't Accept the First Answer

Prompting is a conversation, not a one-shot command. If the first response isn't perfect, refine it.

Refinement phrases to teach students:

  • "Make this more formal/casual"
  • "Expand on the second point"
  • "Now make it shorter — under 100 words"
  • "Give me 3 alternative versions"
  • "That's good, but add an Indian context/example"

The CRISP Framework — Easy to Remember

C — Context

Who are you? What's the situation?

R — Role

Who should AI be? Expert, teacher, friend?

I — Instruction

What exactly do you want done?

S — Specifics

Length, tone, audience, constraints

P — Pattern

Show an example of what you want

Module 4 — 40 to 52 Minutes

Python in 10 Minutes
Zero Fear, Maximum Fun

First code session. Focus: remove fear, build confidence. We don't need to cover everything — we need students to go home wanting to code more.

Before We Code — The Right Mindset

Tell Your Students This

"Python is just English with strict grammar. Instead of saying 'Calculate the average of these numbers', you type sum(numbers) / len(numbers). You already know the logic — Python just has its own spelling rules."

Concept 1: Variables — Labelled Boxes

A variable is like a labelled box. You put something inside, and you can use it later.

Python — Run in Jupyter# Creating variables — like labelling boxes student_name = "Priya" student_age = 22 gpa = 3.8 is_enrolled = True # Using variables print(f"Student: {student_name}, Age: {student_age}, GPA: {gpa}") # Output: Student: Priya, Age: 22, GPA: 3.8 # Python figures out the "type" automatically print(type(student_name)) # <class 'str'> (text) print(type(student_age)) # <class 'int'> (whole number) print(type(gpa)) # <class 'float'> (decimal)
🎓 Classroom Activity — 2 Minutes

Ask students to create 3 variables about themselves (name, city, favourite food) and print them in one sentence. First student to share wins! This simple exercise removes the "coding is scary" barrier immediately.

Concept 2: Lists — Multiple Items in One Box

Python# A list is like a shopping bag — holds multiple items exam_scores = [85, 92, 78, 95, 88] # Access by position (starts at 0!) print(exam_scores[0]) # 85 (first item) print(exam_scores[-1]) # 88 (last item) # Useful built-in functions print(len(exam_scores)) # 5 — how many items print(max(exam_scores)) # 95 — highest score print(min(exam_scores)) # 78 — lowest score print(sum(exam_scores)) # 438 — total # Calculate average (this is actual data science!) average = sum(exam_scores) / len(exam_scores) print(f"Class average: {average}") # 87.6

Concept 3: If-Else — Decision Making

Python# Python can make decisions — just like we do score = 72 if score >= 90: print("Grade: A — Excellent!") elif score >= 75: print("Grade: B — Good job!") elif score >= 60: print("Grade: C — Keep improving") else: print("Grade: F — Please study more") # Output: Grade: C — Keep improving

Concept 4: For Loop — Do Something Repeatedly

Python# A loop does a task multiple times automatically students = ["Arjun", "Meera", "Rahul", "Divya"] for student in students: print(f"Welcome to DS class, {student}!") # Output: # Welcome to DS class, Arjun! # Welcome to DS class, Meera! # Welcome to DS class, Rahul! # Welcome to DS class, Divya! # Real DS use: loop through scores and grade each one scores = [85, 62, 91, 74] for s in scores: grade = "Pass" if s >= 70 else "Fail" print(f"Score {s}: {grade}")

Mini Data Science Project — 5 Lines of Code

Show students they can do real data analysis with what they just learned:

Python — Your First Data Analysis!# A mini data science project with just basic Python monthly_sales = [45000, 52000, 48000, 61000, 55000, 70000, 49000, 67000, 72000, 58000, 63000, 80000] total_revenue = sum(monthly_sales) average_sales = total_revenue / len(monthly_sales) best_month = max(monthly_sales) worst_month = min(monthly_sales) print(f"Total Revenue: ₹{total_revenue:,}") print(f"Monthly Average: ₹{average_sales:,.0f}") print(f"Best Month: ₹{best_month:,}") print(f"Worst Month: ₹{worst_month:,}") # This is EXACTLY what data analysts do every day!

Top 10 Questions Students Will Ask

#QuestionBest Answer
1"Do I need to be good at math?"Basic statistics (mean, percentage) is enough to start. Advanced math comes later, and tools handle most of it.
2"Python or R — which should I learn?"Python. It's industry standard, used for both data science AND general programming. R is niche (academic).
3"Will AI replace data scientists?"AI replaces repetitive tasks, not human judgment. DS/AI engineers who can use AI tools are MORE in demand, not less.

9"What if I get stuck?"That's normal! Stack Overflow, ChatGPT, and the course community are your resources. Getting stuck is how you learn.
10"Is Python hard?"Python is designed to be readable. The first week feels hard because it's new. By week 3, most students are surprised how natural it feels.
